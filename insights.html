AI Program Management · January 2026

How I Scaled AI Feature Adoption to 60% Across an Enterprise Platform

When I joined the Atlassian Growth team, Atlassian Intelligence had a telemetry problem. Not a product problem — the features were solid. The problem was that nobody really knew who was using what, or why the numbers plateaued the way they did. That's where a Technical Program Manager either earns their keep or doesn't.

The first thing I did wasn't a roadmap review. It was a data audit. I spent two weeks mapping what signals actually existed, what was being tracked versus what was being reported to leadership, and where the gaps were. Adoption numbers that look flat are usually a measurement problem before they're a product problem. In this case, we were counting sessions that touched the feature — not sessions where someone got value from it. That's a very different number.

Once we had accurate instrumentation, the adoption story changed fast. We'd been undercounting by about 30%. But even the corrected number needed to grow, so we moved to the actual work: getting the feature into the workflows people were already in. For Atlassian Intelligence in Jira, that meant the Slack integration. Not a new surface — the same one engineers were already living in all day. The integration work was real engineering effort, but the program challenge was getting three separate teams — Jira, Slack ecosystem, and the AI platform team — to sequence their work in a way that didn't leave any of them blocked.

That sequencing problem is where enterprise AI program management actually lives. The technology works. The model works. The gap is almost always cross-functional coordination at the pace that AI product cycles demand, which is faster than most legacy program structures were built for. I ended up restructuring the cross-team sync cadence, cutting the weekly all-hands in favor of async updates and two tighter working sessions per week — one on blockers, one on dependencies. Fewer meetings, faster decisions.

By the end of the engagement we'd hit 60%+ adoption across the integrated surface and increased Confluence document engagement 277% through a parallel Slack strategy. The lesson that stuck: AI adoption is a program management problem wearing a technology costume. The model isn't what moves the number. Alignment, sequencing, and measurement do.

Compliance Program Management · February 2026

The Compliance Program Manager Playbook: Closing Regulatory Issues at Scale

Most engineering teams treat compliance issues the way they treat tech debt — something to acknowledge, defer, and revisit when there's bandwidth. There's rarely bandwidth. So the backlog grows, regulatory commitments slip, and by the time CCO leadership asks for a status update, nobody has a clean answer. I've walked into that situation more than once.

At Intuit, my mandate was to build a regulatory issue lifecycle framework from scratch — one that could handle volume, provide real-time visibility to VP and CCO leadership, and actually close things on time. The first version of the framework had three components: a triage protocol, an aging and severity taxonomy, and an escalation ladder with teeth.

Triage sounds obvious but most teams skip it. Every new regulatory issue that came in got scored on two axes within 48 hours: risk severity and resolution complexity. High severity, low complexity issues moved immediately to a dedicated sprint slot. High severity, high complexity issues got their own workstream and an executive owner. The mistake most compliance programs make is treating every issue as equally urgent — which means none of them are truly urgent, because the team can't distinguish between a critical regulatory gap and a documentation hygiene item.

The aging taxonomy was the second piece. I created four buckets — current, at-risk, overdue, and critical — and tied each to reporting thresholds. At-risk issues surfaced in the weekly dashboard. Overdue triggered a structured escalation. Critical issues got a direct line to VP leadership with a named DRI and a resolution date. The key was that the thresholds were published upfront. No surprises. Teams knew exactly when something would escalate, which created its own accountability.

Within the first two quarters we hit 96%+ on-time closure and reduced the backlog by 31%. That number sounds good in a slide deck, but what actually mattered was that CCO-level reporting went from being a scramble to a 15-minute dashboard pull. Good compliance program management isn't about controls — it's about making the right information available to the right person at the right time. The framework just made that the default.

Growth Program Management · March 2026

From $3M to $13M: What Growth Program Management Actually Looks Like

When people hear "Technical Program Manager on a growth team" they often picture someone running ceremonies and updating Jira tickets. That's not what it is. At Intuit's TurboTax growth portfolio, a TPM was the person who held together a machine that spanned data engineering, product, marketing, and analytics — all moving at different speeds toward the same number. The number, in this case, was funded accounts.

We started at 300,000. The target was 1.3 million. The gap wasn't a product gap — TurboTax was a strong product. The gap was infrastructure. Specifically, the system that matched customer segments to offers couldn't process data fast enough to run the personalization logic at the scale we needed. We were leaving conversions on the table because the pipeline couldn't keep up with the traffic.

The program decision that unlocked everything was an enterprise data bridge — a technical architecture change that let us process real-time tax attributes instead of batching them overnight. I didn't build it, but I owned the program: scope, sequencing, risk, and the executive alignment that kept the engineering investment funded through two competing quarterly planning cycles. That's the part that doesn't show up in an architecture diagram but decides whether the project ships or stalls.

Once the bridge was live, the offer engine could actually personalize at scale. We went from serving a fairly blunt set of bundle offers to running targeted strategies by segment, filing type, and account history. Revenue per cohort went up. Conversion improved. Funded accounts hit 1.3M. Revenue grew from $3M to $13M in the portfolio period, and 2.6M net new customers came through the data modernization work downstream.

The short version of what I learned: growth program management at scale isn't about process. It's about knowing which technical constraint is the real ceiling, and then doing whatever it takes — politically, structurally, operationally — to remove it. The ceremonies come second. The constraint comes first.
